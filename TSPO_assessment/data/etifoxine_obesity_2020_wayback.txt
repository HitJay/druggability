Abstract:
Obesity is intimately associated with diet and dysbiosis of gut microorganisms but
anxiolytics, widely used in treatment of psychiatric conditions, frequently result in
weight gain and associated metabolic disorders. We are interested in effects of the
anxiolytic Etifoxine, which has not been studied with respect to weight gain or effects
on gut microorganisms. Here we induced obesity in mice by feeding a high-fat diet but
found that intraperitoneal administration of Etifoxine resulted in weight loss and
decreased serum cholesterol and triglycerides. Obese mice had increased hepatic
transcripts associated with lipid metabolism (cyp7a1, cyp27a1, abcg1 and LXRα) and
inflammatory factors (TNFα and IL18) but these effects were reversed after Etifoxine
treatment other than cyp7a1. Taxonomic profiles of the organisms from the caecum
were generated by 16S rRNA gene sequencing and Obese and Etifoxine mice show
differences by diversity metrics, Differential Abundance and functional metagenomics.
Organisms in genus Oscillospira and genera from Lachnospiraceae family and
Clostridiales order are higher in Control than Obese and at intermediate levels with
Etifoxine treatment. With respect to community metabolic potential, Etifoxine mice have
characteristics similar to Control and particularly with respect to metabolism of
butanoate, sphingolipid, lipid biosynthesis and xenobiotic metabolism. We suggest
mechanisms where-by Etifoxine influences processes of host, such as on bile acid
synthesis, and microbiota, such as signalling from production of butanoate and
sphingosine, resulting in decreased cholesterol, lipids and inflammatory factors. We
speculate that the indirect effect of Etifoxine on microbial composition is mediated by
microbial b-glucuronidases that metabolise excreted Etifoxine glucuronides.

1. Introduction  
48 
The use of many antipsychotic drugs is compromised by side-effects of obesity [1] and 
49 
associated metabolic syndromes resulting in increased morbidity and mortality [2]. Obesity 
50 
and associated metabolic disorders are a global health concern and causes of obesity are 
51 
complex, but it is widely accepted that diets rich in fat and meat-derived proteins, typical of 
52 
the Western hemisphere, are a major cause [3]. Indeed, the large surges in obesity in many 
53 
developing countries seen in recent decades can be attributed to a shift from a vegetable-rich 
54 
diet to a more Western-style diet. Appetite and food intake are under complex genetic control 
55 
involving the interaction of hormones responding to dietary metabolites, neuro-endocrines 
56 
and gut-produced incretins and Western-style diets appear to be able to over-ride these 
57 
controls [4,5]. The reasons why antipsychotics cause obesity are largely unknown and here 
58 
we investigate the effects of etifoxine, an antipsychotic that has not previously been assessed 
59 
in this respect. 
60 
 
61 
Obesity is associated with dramatic changes of the gut microbiota from communities typical 
62 
of the lean state [7]. Animals are host to complex communities of microorganisms in the gut 
63 
that play a symbiotic role in well-being in which reciprocal signalling between host and 
64 
microbiome are significant [6]. It is postulated that bacterial dysbiosis results in alterations of 
65 
the two-way provision and nature of signalling between host and bacteria. Increased 
66 
production of energy-producing products, of inflammatory products and of bacterial toxins 
67 
along with decreased production of mucin-producing stimulants are significant in the onset of 
68 
obesity [8]. An example of two-way signalling is provided by bile acids which are inter-
69 
related to host cholesterol homeostasis. Excess cholesterol is converted to primary bile acids 
70 
in the liver and these may be converted by gut bacteria to secondary bile acids. In the liver of 
71 
mice, bile acid synthesis is initiated principally by cyp7a1 but also by mitochondrial cyp27a1

Since etifoxine is likely to be a substrate for hepatic xenobiotic-metabolising systems, the

85

86 
experimental system etifoxine is administered intraperitoneally, so will initially interact with 
87 
host systems regulating lipid metabolism and drug metabolism.

2. Materials and Methods 
100 
2.1. Animal Husbandry 
101 
The animals used for this study were housed in the Animal Care Unit of the University 
102 
of Strathclyde, according to the UK Home Office Animal Care regulations and the 
103 
experimental procedures were authorised by the University of Strathclyde Animal Welfare 
104 
Authority (Project licence P8C815DC9). C57BL/6 male mice (body weight, 20-25g at the 
105 
start of the experiment) were used. The mice were randomly divided into three experimental 
106 
groups, 6 animals per group; two groups were fed with a high-fat diet (HFD, Crude fat 22.3%, 
107 
Crude protein 19.9%, Crude fibre 3.8%, Ash 5.1%, Carbohydrate 41.1% (w/w); provided by 
108 
Special Diet Services, UK) while the third group was fed with a standard chow diet (7% 
109 
simple sugars, 3% fat, 50% polysaccharide, 15% protein (w/w) (Special Diet Services, UK). 
110 
After 13 weeks, one group of HFD-fed mice were treated daily with etifoxine (50mg/kg), i.p. 
111 
and the other two groups were treated with 0.1% Tween-80 in saline solution (vehicle of 
112 
etifoxine) for 15 days after which the mice were sacrificed by anaesthesia (CO2) and serum 
113 
and liver collected. Note that the dosage used here is at the low end of that used in human 
114 
treatment [17,18].  Luminal samples were extracted from the colon adjacent to the caecum 
115 
and then frozen in dry ice and stored at -80⁰ C until used.

2.5. 16S rRNA gene library production and sequencing 
143 
Purified DNA from each animal was used for PCR amplification and sequencing of 
144 
16S rRNA genes on an Illumina MiSeq instrument with 2 x 300 base-pair paired-end reads at 
145 
LGC Genomics, Germany. Universal primers of 16S rRNA genes were used to amplify the 
146

147 
TruSeq adapters and tag index sequences were attached prior to sequencing. Reads were 
148 
demultiplexed at LGC and basic statistics are shown in Table 2. All reads were reported as 
149 
fastq along with fastqc files showing phred values > 20. Reads and metadata have been 
150 
submitted to the SRA with Accession Number PRJNA596121.

3. Results 
195 
Mice were maintained either on a normal diet (referred to as CD or Control group) or 
196 
one containing high fat (referred to as HFD or Obese group) for a period of 13 weeks. After 
197 
this time the animals were maintained on their original diet for another 15 days but a subset of 
198 
the HFD mice were also administered etifoxine (E), i.p., (referred to as HFD+E, Etifoxine 
199 
group). During this phase the other HFD and Control animals received vehicle treatment. 
200 
Data recorded over the initial 13 weeks showed that animals on the HFD diet gained 
201 
considerable weight relative to the Controls (Figure 1a). Those animals kept on the high-fat 
202 
diet and vehicle continued this trajectory during the second phase of the experiment while 
203 
those on HFD+E showed weight loss as early as 7 days and this became significant at 13 days 
204 
and continued to the end of the experiment (Figure 1b). Control animals showed expected, 
205 
continuous, modest weight gain through both stages of the experiment.

357

358

4.4. How does etifoxine affect gut microbiota? 
431 
Few studies have investigated the effects of drugs on gut microbiota other than by direct 
432 
dietary provision [57–59] but here we find that bacterial communities are affected by the i.p. 
433 
administration of etifoxine and metabolic functional potential would be altered raising the 
434 
question of the mechanism behind this effect. 
435 
We had expected to discover the significant interplay between cholesterol metabolism 
436 
in the host  with bile acids in the gut since these influence the composition of gut bacterial 
437 
communities [60]. Although distinctions were found between groups and some KEGG 
438 
orthologous enzymes, there was no difference at the pathway level. Another possible 
439 
explanation of the indirect effect on gut microbiota is that metabolites of etifoxine influence 
440 
the communities. Etifoxine is metabolised in the liver by de-ethylation, oxidation and 
441 
subsequent glucuronidation [61]. It is likely that the action of bacterial -glucuronidase will 
442 
release glucuronide and an etifoxine metabolite (desethyl hydroxyl etifoxine). Either may play 
443 
a role in reshaping the bacterial communities by the provision of an energy source and by 
444 
unknown signalling from the aglycone. Significantly with regard to these suggestions imputed 
445 
-glucuronidase activity was found to be lower in HFD than in CD or HFD+E and taxa of 
446 
Clostridiales and Lachnospiraceae appear to make a higher contribution to these activities. 
447 
The importance of bacterial -glucuronidase action in weight loss has been previously 
448 
referred to [62]. 
449

450 
on host and gut microbiota contribute to this as would be, imputed, improved lipid 
451 
metabolism and decreased production of virulence factors. In our model obesity was 
452 
associated with decreased signalling to the gut by butanoate and sphingolipids but increased 
453 
production of inflammatory agents and lipids usable for host metabolism. We propose that 
454 
signalling from host to bacteria is mediated in part by etifoxine glucuronide which is a
